## <Highlighter>What Reactive Tinnitus Actually Means</Highlighter>

Reactive tinnitus is the pattern where external sound makes your tinnitus louder, sharper, or more distorted instead of covering it, either during the sound or for a while after it stops. It is a description rather than a formal diagnosis, and it usually travels with reduced tolerance for everyday sound.

You turn on the shower expecting relief and the hiss climbs on top of the water. You leave a restaurant and the ring is twice what it was walking in, then takes two hours to come back down. Standard advice tells you to use background sound, and background sound seems to be the problem.

You are not doing it wrong, and you are not imagining it. This guide covers what is happening in the auditory system, which triggers matter most, and a graded plan that lowers reactivity instead of feeding it. If the underlying noise is still new to you, start with **[what is tinnitus](/blog/what-is-tinnitus)**.

## <Highlighter>Is Reactive Tinnitus A Real Condition?</Highlighter>

You will not find reactive tinnitus in a clinical guideline, which is why searching for it turns up forum threads rather than medical pages. What clinicians do recognise is **decreased sound tolerance**, and reactivity is one of the ways it shows up. The label is still useful, because it describes something specific that generic tinnitus advice does not address.

It helps to separate three experiences that get mixed together.

| Experience | What happens with sound | Typical after-effect | Usual overlap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary masking | Background sound partly covers the tinnitus | Tinnitus returns to baseline when sound stops | Most people with tinnitus |
| Reactive tinnitus | Tinnitus rises, sharpens, or distorts alongside the sound | Stays elevated for minutes to hours | Common with sound intolerance |
| Hyperacusis | The external sound itself feels too loud or painful | Discomfort or pain, sometimes lingering | Frequently present together |

Reactivity and loudness discomfort often sit in the same person, which is why the plans overlap heavily with the approach in **[hyperacusis and tinnitus](/blog/hyperacusis-and-tinnitus)**. If your main complaint is that sound hurts rather than that the ring climbs, that is the more useful starting point.

## <Highlighter>Why Sound Amplifies The Ringing Instead Of Hiding It</Highlighter>

Two mechanisms explain most of what you are noticing, and neither of them means new damage.

The first is **central gain**. When the ear sends up reduced or patchy input, the brain compensates by turning up its own amplification, the way a microphone with the gain cranked picks up hiss along with the voice. Incoming sound arrives boosted, and the internal signal rides up with it. Because the amplification is set centrally, adding external sound does not subtract from the tinnitus. It pushes the whole system harder. The circuitry behind this is covered in **[the role of neural pathways in tinnitus perception](/blog/the-role-of-neural-pathways-in-tinnitus-perception)**.

The second is **threat learning**. Once your brain has tagged sound as the thing that makes the ring worse, it starts monitoring for it. Attention locks on, the stress response fires before the noise is even loud, and both the external sound and the internal one get promoted in priority. This is the part that responds best to treatment, and the reason reactivity can drop substantially without any change in your hearing test.

Loud exposure sets the whole system on edge, which is why a flare so often starts after a concert, a power tool, or a run of noisy days. Hearing loss, jaw and neck tension, migraine physiology, and some medications can all raise baseline gain too. Short-term swings usually have a trigger behind them, and the common ones are mapped out in **[why does tinnitus spike](/blog/why-does-tinnitus-spike)**.

## <Highlighter>The Triggers People Report Most</Highlighter>

Reactivity is rarely about volume alone. The character of the sound matters as much as its level.

- Broadband hissing sounds close to your tinnitus pitch: showers, taps, extractor fans, air conditioning, road noise.
- Sudden or unpredictable sound: dishes, doors, dogs, keyboard clatter, children.
- Layered noise with speech in it, like an open-plan office or a busy cafe, where your brain is working hard to separate voices.
- Compressed audio through earbuds at moderate volume for long stretches.
- Long silence beforehand, which raises gain and makes the next sound land harder. This is the trap unpacked in **[can silence make tinnitus worse](/blog/can-silence-make-tinnitus-worse)**.

Two things amplify all of the above: short sleep and a stressed nervous system. Reactivity that felt manageable on Tuesday can be intolerable on Friday with nothing in your ears having changed.

## <Highlighter>A Step By Step Plan To Lower Reactivity</Highlighter>

The goal is not to find a sound that masks the tinnitus. With reactivity, that search usually fails and raises frustration. The goal is to lower the gain, and that means keeping safe sound present at levels your system accepts, then widening the range slowly.

1) Find your floor, not your mask

Set background sound at the quietest level you can still detect, well below your tinnitus, and leave it there. Do not chase coverage. Neutral, steady, low-information tracks work best: try **[White Noise](/zen/white-noise)** or **[Nature Sounds](/zen/nature-sounds)** and drop the volume until it is barely there. The logic behind a floor rather than a blanket is in **[the power of white noise](/blog/the-power-of-white-noise)**.

2) Stop the all-day protection

Keep earplugs for genuinely loud exposure and take them out everywhere else. Constant protection in ordinary rooms teaches the system that normal life is loud, so reactivity climbs. Active noise cancelling is worth the same caution, and the trade-offs are covered in **[noise-canceling headphones for tinnitus](/blog/noise-canceling-headphones-for-tinnitus)**.

3) Build a ladder

List your triggers from easiest to worst. Start two steps below the easiest, in five to ten minute exposures, once or twice a day. Add duration before you add intensity, and only change one variable at a time.

4) Give it weeks, and log it

Reactivity moves slowly and unevenly, so day-to-day readings mislead. Note the trigger, the level, the after-effect, and how long it took to settle. A few weeks of entries will show a trend that memory flattens out, and tracking it in **[our own app](/app)** keeps the record honest.

5) Protect sleep before you touch anything else

Short sleep raises gain more reliably than almost any sound exposure, so a week of decent nights often does more for reactivity than a new device. The practical starting points are in **[tinnitus and sleep](/blog/tinnitus-and-sleep)**.

6) Work on the threat response directly

Because part of reactivity is learned prediction, the therapy that targets prediction helps. Structured approaches have the strongest evidence base of anything in tinnitus care, and what they involve is set out in **[cognitive behavioral therapy and tinnitus](/blog/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-and-tinnitus)**. The anxiety side of the loop is worth addressing in parallel, as in **[tinnitus and anxiety: staying mentally strong](/blog/tinnitus-and-anxiety-staying-mentally-strong)**.

## <Highlighter>Mistakes That Keep Reactivity High</Highlighter>

Most stalled progress comes down to a handful of habits, and all of them are understandable responses to a frightening symptom.

##### The avoidance spiral

- Cancelling social plans, then finding the next outing worse, which confirms the fear and shrinks the range further.
- Living in near-silence at home, which raises gain and makes every unavoidable sound land harder.
- Wearing protection preemptively for sounds that are not actually loud.

##### The testing habit

- Checking the tinnitus repeatedly to see whether it reacted, which keeps attention locked on it.
- Trying a new masking sound every few days, so nothing gets a fair trial.
- Judging progress by the worst hour of the week rather than the trend across a month.

##### The overcorrection

- Jumping several rungs up the ladder after a good day, then retreating after the flare.
- Pushing through pain rather than backing off one step and resuming the next day.
- Treating a spike as proof of damage. Spikes are the normal shape of recovery, not evidence against it.

Steady, boring consistency beats intensity here, and the broader habit framework in **[managing tinnitus](/blog/managing-tinnitus)** applies directly.

## <Highlighter>When To Get It Checked</Highlighter>

Reactivity itself is usually not an emergency, but some of the things that trigger it need attention on a clock.

##### Seek urgent care if

- Your hearing drops suddenly, especially in one ear.
- Tinnitus appears on one side only, or pulses in time with your heartbeat.
- You have spinning vertigo, discharge, bleeding, or severe ear pain.
- Reactivity started immediately after a blast or a head injury.

##### Book an appointment soon if

- Sound avoidance is affecting your work, study, or relationships.
- You are using ear protection for most of your waking hours.
- Reactivity has been climbing steadily for more than a few weeks despite a sensible plan.

Ask specifically for loudness discomfort level testing alongside a standard hearing test, and for a graded desensitisation plan rather than reassurance alone. Untreated hearing loss is worth ruling out early, since correcting input is one of the few things that lowers gain from the bottom up. What a full workup involves is described in **[how audiologists diagnose and treat tinnitus](/blog/how-audiologists-diagnose-and-treat-tinnitus)**.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: Reactivity Is The Part That Moves</Highlighter>

Reactive tinnitus feels like a closed door, because the one tool everyone recommends appears to make things worse. It is not a closed door. It is a system running at high gain, and gain is adjustable. The people who improve are almost always the ones who stopped hunting for a sound loud enough to win and started keeping a quiet, steady floor instead.

Expect the curve to be bumpy. A flare after a noisy afternoon is not a setback in the plan, it is the plan working at the edge of your current range. What you are looking for over a month is a shorter recovery time, not a quieter week.

Pick two changes today: set your background sound to barely audible, and take the earplugs out for everything that is not genuinely loud. Add the ladder once those two feel routine, and book the hearing test if you have not had one recently.
